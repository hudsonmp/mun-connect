# UN GENERAL ASSEMBLY: CLIMATE CHANGE AND GLOBAL RESPONSE
## BACKGROUND GUIDE

# INTRODUCTION

This background guide is designed to provide delegates with a comprehensive overview of the issue of climate change, its global impact, and the international community's response. Climate change represents one of the most pressing challenges facing our planet today, with far-reaching implications for ecosystems, economies, and human societies worldwide.

The General Assembly, as the main deliberative organ of the United Nations, plays a crucial role in addressing global challenges such as climate change. It provides a forum for member states to discuss and develop strategies for collective action. While the General Assembly's resolutions are not legally binding, they carry significant moral and political weight, reflecting the consensus of the international community.

Delegates are encouraged to approach this topic with a thorough understanding of their assigned country's position on climate change, including their national policies, international commitments, and particular vulnerabilities. This background guide aims to provide a foundation for such understanding, outlining key aspects of the climate change debate and highlighting potential areas for international cooperation.

# HISTORICAL CONTEXT

Climate change has been on the international agenda since the late 20th century. The scientific community's growing concern about the impact of human activities on the Earth's climate system led to the establishment of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) in 1988. The IPCC's first assessment report in 1990 provided scientific evidence of human-induced climate change, setting the stage for international action.

The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), adopted at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro in 1992, marked the first major international effort to address climate change. The UNFCCC established a framework for climate action with the ultimate objective of stabilizing greenhouse gas concentrations "at a level that would prevent dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system."

The Kyoto Protocol, adopted in 1997, built upon the UNFCCC by setting binding emission reduction targets for developed countries. However, the protocol faced significant challenges, including the non-participation of the United States and later withdrawals by Canada, Japan, and Russia.

The Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015, represents the most recent and comprehensive international climate treaty. Unlike the Kyoto Protocol, the Paris Agreement includes commitments from both developed and developing countries, with each nation determining its own contribution to the global climate effort. The agreement aims to limit global warming to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, with efforts to limit the increase to 1.5°C.

# CURRENT SITUATION

The latest scientific evidence confirms that climate change is accelerating. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report, released in 2021, describes climate change as "widespread, rapid, and intensifying." The report indicates that many changes are unprecedented over thousands of years and that some impacts, such as sea-level rise, are now irreversible over hundreds to thousands of years.

Global greenhouse gas emissions continue to rise, despite international commitments to reduce them. The concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere has reached levels not seen in at least 800,000 years. The decade 2011-2020 was the warmest on record, with global average temperatures approximately 1.1°C above pre-industrial levels.

The impacts of climate change are being felt across the globe, though not equally. Rising sea levels threaten coastal communities and small island states. Extreme weather events, including hurricanes, floods, and wildfires, are becoming more frequent and severe. Droughts and changing precipitation patterns affect agricultural productivity, threatening food security in many regions. Biodiversity loss is accelerating as ecosystems struggle to adapt to changing conditions.

Developing countries, particularly the least developed countries and small island developing states, are disproportionately affected by climate change, despite having contributed the least to greenhouse gas emissions. This raises important questions of climate justice and equity.

# INTERNATIONAL RESPONSE

The Paris Agreement remains the centerpiece of the international response to climate change. Under the agreement, countries have submitted Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), outlining their climate actions. However, current NDCs are insufficient to meet the Paris Agreement's temperature goals, with projections suggesting a warming of approximately 2.7°C by the end of the century.

The COP26 climate summit in Glasgow in 2021 sought to increase global ambition, resulting in the Glasgow Climate Pact. The pact calls on countries to strengthen their 2030 emission reduction targets by the end of 2022, to commit to phasing down coal power and phasing out inefficient fossil fuel subsidies, and to double finance for adaptation.

Climate finance remains a critical issue, with developed countries falling short of their commitment to mobilize $100 billion per year by 2020 to support climate action in developing countries. The establishment of a loss and damage funding mechanism to address irreversible climate impacts has also been a contentious point in international negotiations.

Beyond the UNFCCC process, other international forums and initiatives are addressing climate change. The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 13 on climate action, embed climate change in the broader development agenda. The G7 and G20 have made climate commitments, and various sectoral initiatives focus on areas such as renewable energy, deforestation, and methane emissions.

# KEY CHALLENGES

Several key challenges confront the international community's response to climate change:

## Ambition Gap
Current national climate pledges fall short of what is needed to avoid dangerous climate change. Increasing the ambition of NDCs and ensuring their implementation is crucial.

## Just Transition
Shifting to a low-carbon economy involves significant economic and social transformations. Ensuring that this transition is just and equitable, particularly for workers and communities dependent on carbon-intensive industries, is essential.

## Climate Finance
Mobilizing adequate financial resources to support mitigation and adaptation, particularly in developing countries, remains a challenge. This includes public finance, private investment, and innovative financing mechanisms.

## Technology Transfer
Facilitating the development and transfer of climate-friendly technologies is key to enabling all countries to pursue low-carbon development paths.

## Adaptation and Resilience
As some climate impacts are now unavoidable, enhancing adaptive capacity and building resilience is increasingly important, especially for the most vulnerable populations.

## Loss and Damage
Addressing the irreversible impacts of climate change, including economic and non-economic losses, requires dedicated attention and resources.

## Governance and Coordination
Ensuring coherent and coordinated action across various levels of governance and different international forums is a persistent challenge.

# POTENTIAL AREAS FOR ACTION

Delegates should consider the following potential areas for action:

## Enhanced NDCs
Strengthening national climate pledges to align with the 1.5°C goal, including more ambitious 2030 targets and long-term strategies for achieving net-zero emissions.

## Climate Finance
Increasing the scale and accessibility of climate finance, with a focus on adaptation and the needs of the most vulnerable countries. This might involve innovative financing mechanisms and private sector mobilization.

## Technology and Capacity Building
Enhancing international cooperation on technology development and transfer, as well as building the capacity of developing countries to implement climate actions.

## Adaptation Framework
Developing a comprehensive framework for adaptation, including metrics for assessing progress and mechanisms for sharing best practices.

## Loss and Damage
Establishing concrete mechanisms to address loss and damage, potentially including a dedicated fund, insurance schemes, or debt relief for climate-vulnerable countries.

## Just Transition
Creating international guidelines and support mechanisms for ensuring that the transition to a low-carbon economy is just and equitable.

## Climate and Security
Addressing the security implications of climate change, including potential conflicts over resources and climate-induced displacement.

## Multi-stakeholder Engagement
Enhancing the participation of non-state actors, including civil society, the private sector, and local communities, in the international climate regime.

# CONCLUSION

Climate change represents a defining challenge of our time, requiring unprecedented global cooperation. The General Assembly, with its universal membership and broad mandate, has a vital role to play in advancing the international response to climate change.

Delegates are encouraged to engage constructively with this complex issue, seeking common ground while respecting the diverse perspectives and circumstances of different countries. By working together, the international community can develop effective strategies to address climate change, protect the planet for future generations, and ensure a sustainable and prosperous future for all.
